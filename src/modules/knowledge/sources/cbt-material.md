# A Course in CBT Techniques: A Free Online CBT Workbook

## Overview

The material presents cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as a short-term, skills-based psychotherapy approach focused on developing greater influence over thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. The workbook teaches CBT through sequential modules, exercises, and worksheets that can be practiced independently or alongside a qualified CBT therapist. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The central CBT model describes thoughts, feelings, and behaviors as interconnected components that continually influence one another. Changes in one component can produce changes in the others. The workbook progressively teaches goal setting, mood monitoring, identification of automatic thoughts, cognitive restructuring, recognition of cognitive distortions, opposite action, behavioral activation, and exposure. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Key Concepts

- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT):** A short-term, skill-based psychotherapy approach that teaches people to exert greater influence over thoughts, feelings, and behaviors in order to address life challenges. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}
- **CBT cognitive model:** Situations do not independently determine emotional and behavioral outcomes; reactions, particularly interpretations of situations, play an important role. Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact continuously. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}
- **Cognitive triangle:** Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors form an interconnected cycle. A change in one component can trigger changes in the others. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}
- **Automatic thoughts:** Thoughts that arise automatically throughout the day and are often outside conscious awareness. They can appear as words, sentences, single words, or mental images. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}
- **Metacognition:** Awareness and examination of one's own thinking process. Recognizing thoughts as thoughts can create distance from reflexive cognitive responses and facilitate reevaluation. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}
- **Cognitive restructuring:** The process of identifying ineffective thinking patterns and modifying them toward thinking that produces less negative emotion, clearer perception, or more effective behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}
- **Cognitive distortions:** Recurring mental habits that can cause situations to be interpreted in ways that are inconsistent with available facts or omit important information. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}
- **Opposite action:** Deliberately engaging in behavior opposite to an emotional urge when the emotion is unhelpful or excessively intense. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}
- **Behavioral activation:** A CBT treatment for clinical depression that increases engagement in pleasurable and mastery-oriented activities instead of following mood-dependent avoidance. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}
- **Exposure:** A behavioral approach for anxiety that interrupts avoidance cycles by repeatedly approaching previously avoided feared situations. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

## Detailed Summary

### Structure and Use of the Workbook

The workbook consists of eight sequential parts followed by a collection of printable worksheets. The sequence progresses from understanding the CBT model to goal setting, mood monitoring, automatic thoughts, cognitive restructuring, cognitive distortions, and behavioral techniques. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

CBT is presented as a learnable skills-based approach. Much of the benefit is described as coming from practicing techniques between sessions, including recording thoughts, testing predictions, scheduling activities, and approaching avoided situations. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

For self-guided use, the workbook recommends progressing sequentially, working slowly, completing the worksheets rather than only reading, and writing observations down. The modules are intended to build upon one another. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

The material distinguishes a self-help workbook from accredited professional training. The workbook provides CBT concepts, techniques, and worksheets but is not presented as an accredited training program and does not provide a certificate. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}

### CBT Model of Thoughts, Feelings, and Behaviors

The CBT model emphasizes that a situation itself is generally not the sole determinant of whether it becomes problematic. The way a person reacts to and interprets the situation influences the resulting experience. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}

Thoughts influence how situations are experienced emotionally and behaviorally. The same external event can therefore produce substantially different reactions depending on the interpretation assigned to it. :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}

Behavior also affects thoughts and feelings. Avoidance can prevent a person from gathering evidence that challenges negative expectations, which can reinforce those expectations and lead to further avoidance. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}

Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors continuously influence one another. A strong emotion can affect subsequent thoughts, while those thoughts can influence behavior. Changes to one component can therefore produce a chain reaction across the other components. :contentReference[oaicite:19]{index=19}

The workbook uses examples involving depression, panic, and sadness to demonstrate these feedback loops. Negative thoughts can increase unpleasant feelings and promote avoidance; avoidance can then reinforce the original thoughts. Conversely, behavioral changes and less negative interpretations can interrupt these cycles. :contentReference[oaicite:20]{index=20} :contentReference[oaicite:21]{index=21}

### Developing Insight

The workbook emphasizes distinguishing thoughts, feelings, and behaviors during emotionally difficult situations. Breaking an emotional experience into these components can reveal which thoughts or behaviors are maintaining or worsening the problem and where intervention can occur. :contentReference[oaicite:22]{index=22}

Practice involves selecting situations involving strong or uncomfortable emotions and identifying their cognitive, physiological, and behavioral components. Initially, the exercise can be completed after the situation has passed; with practice, identifying the components can become easier during the situation itself. :contentReference[oaicite:23]{index=23}

### Goal Setting

The workbook recommends first developing an objective and concrete description of the problem. Instead of describing an issue only with broad statements such as being unhappy or unable to cope, the person is encouraged to specify what the problem looks like in daily life and identify associated thoughts, emotions, and actions. :contentReference[oaicite:24]{index=24}

The goal-setting process begins with broad concerns and then converts them into specific, observable objectives. Examples of general concerns include low mood, low self-esteem, shame, anxiety, low productivity, anger, habits a person wants to change, and difficulty asserting oneself. :contentReference[oaicite:25]{index=25}

Goals are developed in three stages:

1. Define the ultimate goal associated with each general problem.
2. Make the goal specific enough that it is possible to determine whether it has been achieved.
3. Identify actions that indicate movement toward the goal. :contentReference[oaicite:26]{index=26}

The resulting goals function as a plan or blueprint for the later CBT work. :contentReference[oaicite:27]{index=27}

### Monitoring Moods

The workbook describes moods as variable in intensity and capable of involving different thoughts and physical sensations. Multiple emotions can occur simultaneously, while at other times emotions may feel difficult to identify or appear emotionally numb. :contentReference[oaicite:28]{index=28}

The material distinguishes emotions from thoughts. Statements beginning with “I feel” may actually express thoughts rather than emotions. Emotions are presented as one-word experiences belonging to five broad categories: fear, joy, anger, disgust, and sadness. :contentReference[oaicite:29]{index=29}

Emotions are described as serving two principal functions:

- They provide signals to the individual and can motivate action.
- They communicate information to other people and can motivate their responses. :contentReference[oaicite:30]{index=30}

Emotional signals are not always accurate indicators of an actual need for action. Fear, for example, can occur without actual danger. Therefore, emotional signals should not automatically be treated as facts, although they can provide information. :contentReference[oaicite:31]{index=31}

Mood monitoring involves recording the situation, identifying emotions, rating their peak intensity from 1 to 10, and noting accompanying physical sensations such as changes in breathing, heartbeat, tension, sweating, shaking, nausea, dizziness, or temperature. :contentReference[oaicite:32]{index=32}

The workbook recommends tracking moods for a full week, preferably consistently each day, to increase awareness and identify patterns. :contentReference[oaicite:33]{index=33}

### Automatic Thoughts

Automatic thoughts arise spontaneously and frequently outside conscious awareness. Identifying them can help clarify the relationship between thinking, mood, and behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:34]{index=34}

The material states that the mind processes large numbers of thoughts while filtering information and assigning meaning to selected aspects of situations. Problems can arise when relevant information is filtered out or meaning is assigned without sufficient grounding in the facts. :contentReference[oaicite:35]{index=35}

Automatic thoughts can strongly influence emotional responses. Two people can receive identical information yet experience different emotions and behave differently because their automatic interpretations differ. :contentReference[oaicite:36]{index=36}

Automatic thoughts may appear as:

- words or complete sentences;
- single words;
- mental images. :contentReference[oaicite:37]{index=37}

To identify an automatic thought, the workbook recommends asking what was going through the person's mind when an emotion arose. If no obvious thought is accessible, the person can examine the meaning of the situation, ask what the worst part is and why, or work backward from the emotion to infer the associated thought. :contentReference[oaicite:38]{index=38}

### Thought Records

A thought record is a CBT tool for clarifying thoughts associated with unwanted feelings and behaviors. Repeated use is intended to strengthen metacognitive ability and make it easier to recognize dysfunctional automatic thoughts in the moment. :contentReference[oaicite:39]{index=39}

The basic thought-record process is:

1. Describe the situation objectively in one sentence or less.
2. Identify the emotions experienced.
3. Rate the intensity of each emotion from 0 to 100.
4. Identify the thoughts present at the time and rate belief in each thought from 0 to 100.
5. Repeat the exercise regularly, with the material recommending daily practice and at least three or four completed records before progressing. :contentReference[oaicite:40]{index=40}

### Cognitive Restructuring

Cognitive restructuring focuses on changing ineffective thinking patterns rather than simply replacing negative thoughts with positive ones. The intended result is a more sophisticated perspective that takes both positive and negative information into account and produces less negative emotion or more effective behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:41]{index=41}

The method contains four major stages:

1. **Record thoughts and feelings.** Identify the situation and as many associated thoughts as possible.
2. **Select one thought.** Choose the automatic thought most strongly associated with the distress or strongest emotion and convert it into a clear statement.
3. **Develop different perspectives.** Examine the thought using several probing questions.
4. **Create an alternative response.** Formulate a statement that summarizes the most useful conclusions from the investigation. :contentReference[oaicite:42]{index=42} :contentReference[oaicite:43]{index=43}

The probing questions include examining the consequences of believing or not believing the thought, evaluating evidence for and against it, considering alternative explanations, examining worst-, best-, and most-likely outcomes, considering what advice would be given to a friend, and identifying possible actions. :contentReference[oaicite:44]{index=44} :contentReference[oaicite:45]{index=45}

When considering feared outcomes, the workbook recommends making the worst-case scenario specific, considering whether it could be survived and how it could be managed, then considering the best-case and most-likely scenarios. The most-likely scenario is intended to be based on available evidence rather than simply positioned halfway between the extremes. :contentReference[oaicite:46]{index=46}

The “friend” question is used to create distance from personal biases and access a perspective that may be easier to apply to another person than to oneself. :contentReference[oaicite:47]{index=47}

Problem solving is addressed by first brainstorming possible solutions and then selecting and implementing the most effective feasible options. During brainstorming, the workbook recommends generating possibilities without evaluating them until the list is complete. :contentReference[oaicite:48]{index=48}

The alternative response is intended to coexist with the original automatic thought rather than necessarily eliminating it. Repeatedly recalling the alternative response can make it easier to access naturally. :contentReference[oaicite:49]{index=49}

### Cognitive Distortions

Cognitive distortions are described as habitual thinking patterns that can lead to unhelpful interpretations, conflict with available facts, or omit relevant information. Mental shortcuts themselves are not inherently problematic; they become distortions when applied ineffectively. :contentReference[oaicite:50]{index=50}

The workbook identifies these major patterns:

- **Jumping to conclusions / fortune telling:** Predicting future outcomes without knowing what will happen.
- **Mind reading:** Assuming knowledge of another person's thoughts or judgments.
- **Catastrophizing:** Predicting an outcome so terrible that it is viewed as impossible to manage.
- **All-or-nothing thinking:** Treating situations as having only two opposing possibilities with nothing between them.
- **Negative filtering:** Focusing on negative evidence while minimizing or excluding positive evidence.
- **Overgeneralizing:** Drawing broad conclusions from a single event or limited information.
- **Should-ing:** Imposing rules about how oneself, others, or the world ought to be.
- **Labeling:** Using a single trait or characteristic to summarize an entire person. :contentReference[oaicite:51]{index=51}

Countering distortions is presented as an effort to develop more balanced and flexible thinking rather than replacing negative thoughts with positive extremes. :contentReference[oaicite:52]{index=52}

The suggested approaches include considering multiple possible outcomes, estimating their likelihoods, examining coping resources, looking for intermediate possibilities, actively seeking evidence that contradicts negative filtering, identifying exceptions to overgeneralizations, questioning “should” rules, and identifying information excluded by labels. :contentReference[oaicite:53]{index=53}

The workbook recommends identifying distortions in thought records after identifying automatic thoughts and before applying the probing questions from cognitive restructuring. One thought may fit several distortion categories. :contentReference[oaicite:54]{index=54}

### Opposite Action

Opposite action uses behavior that conflicts with an emotional urge in order to reduce the intensity of an overwhelming or unhelpful emotion. The approach is based on the reciprocal influence between behavior and emotional experience. :contentReference[oaicite:55]{index=55}

The process consists of:

1. Determine whether the current emotion is useful or counterproductive in the situation.
2. Identify the behavioral urge associated with the emotion.
3. Perform the opposite behavior persistently until the emotion noticeably decreases. :contentReference[oaicite:56]{index=56}

The workbook associates anger with aggressive engagement, disgust with avoidance or pushing away, sadness with withdrawal and isolation, fear with avoidance or escape, and joy with social and activity engagement. :contentReference[oaicite:57]{index=57}

Examples of opposite behaviors include taking a break instead of acting angrily, approaching rather than avoiding a feared or disgusting stimulus, engaging in pleasurable or social activities during sadness, and grounding oneself physically during excessive excitement. :contentReference[oaicite:58]{index=58}

### Behavioral Activation

Behavioral activation is presented as a CBT treatment for clinical depression. It differs from opposite action in that opposite action is intended primarily for momentary intense emotions, whereas behavioral activation is designed for clinical depression. :contentReference[oaicite:59]{index=59}

The process involves:

1. Track daily activities for one week and rate each activity for pleasure and mastery on a 1–10 scale.
2. Identify and remove activities that consume energy without providing meaningful pleasure or mastery.
3. Generate a broad list of pleasurable and mastery-oriented activities.
4. Schedule selected activities on the calendar for the following week.
5. Complete the scheduled activities and review the results at the end of the week. :contentReference[oaicite:60]{index=60}

The workbook emphasizes that behavioral activation requires continuing with scheduled activities even when motivation is low. Improvement is described as gradual, and the material states that a full course commonly takes two to four months and is generally more effective when performed with guidance from an experienced CBT therapist. :contentReference[oaicite:61]{index=61}

### Exposure for Anxiety

Exposure is described as a behavioral technique that interrupts anxiety-maintaining avoidance. Avoidance can prevent a person from gathering evidence against fearful expectations, thereby strengthening those expectations and increasing anxiety. :contentReference[oaicite:62]{index=62}

Repeatedly approaching feared situations is presented as a way of becoming more accustomed to them. According to the source, this can be followed by changes in thoughts, increased confidence, and greater willingness to approach rather than avoid. :contentReference[oaicite:63]{index=63}

The exposure process consists of:

1. List feared situations, preferably within one specific anxiety area.
2. Arrange them from least to most anxiety-provoking to create an exposure hierarchy.
3. Select an item, create a thought record, and use cognitive restructuring to examine the associated automatic thoughts.
4. Schedule exposure sessions lasting at least 30 minutes and occurring at least three or four times per week, using the same exposure item during the week.
5. Repeat the exposure and move to the next hierarchy item when the selected trigger becomes more manageable. :contentReference[oaicite:64]{index=64}

The source states that more severe anxiety or anxiety with substantial negative life impact is a reason to conduct exposure with a trained CBT therapist. It also explicitly states that opposite action should not be used for situations that are actually dangerous. :contentReference[oaicite:65]{index=65}

### Additional CBT Approaches

The material also includes several shorter CBT-oriented techniques.

**Pie-chart technique for feeling overwhelmed:** identify all sources of stress, assign each a percentage representing its contribution to overall stress, select the largest contributor, break it into smaller steps, and complete the steps individually. :contentReference[oaicite:66]{index=66}

**Square breathing:** gradually slow inhalation and exhalation, optionally introduce pauses, and continue the practice for 10–15 minutes. The source describes the technique as intended to increase relaxation and reduce anxiety and states that twice-daily practice was reported to lower overall anxiety in people prone to excessive worry. :contentReference[oaicite:67]{index=67}

**Mindfulness for pain:** the described approach involves noticing pain rather than struggling against it, observing its physical characteristics, and describing the experience without adding catastrophizing or negative judgment. :contentReference[oaicite:68]{index=68}

**Friend-perspective technique:** when experiencing self-defeating thoughts, consider what advice would be given to a friend facing the same situation. The technique is presented as a way to create psychological distance and obtain a more realistic perspective. :contentReference[oaicite:69]{index=69}

**Half-smile:** the source describes maintaining a subtle smile for approximately ten minutes as a technique intended to influence mood through the relationship between behavior and emotion. It explicitly states that the technique is not a replacement for medication or therapy. :contentReference[oaicite:70]{index=70}

## Practical Approach

1. Begin by identifying the general problem or concern.
2. Describe the problem objectively and specifically, including associated thoughts, emotions, and behaviors.
3. Define an ultimate goal and convert it into specific, observable goals.
4. Identify actions that correspond to those goals.
5. Learn to distinguish thoughts, feelings, and behaviors within difficult situations.
6. Track moods and their physical manifestations over time.
7. Identify automatic thoughts associated with emotional reactions.
8. Use thought records to document situations, emotions, their intensity, and associated thoughts.
9. Select particularly distressing automatic thoughts for cognitive restructuring.
10. Examine their consequences, evidence for and against them, alternative explanations, possible outcomes, advice to a friend, and possible actions.
11. Formulate an alternative response based on the resulting perspectives.
12. Identify recurring cognitive distortions and examine them using balanced and flexible alternatives.
13. When an emotion is unhelpful or excessively intense, identify its behavioral urge and consider an opposite action.
14. For behavioral activation, track activities, identify energy drains, create a pleasure/mastery list, schedule activities, and complete them regardless of current motivation.
15. For exposure, create a hierarchy of feared situations and repeatedly approach selected items according to the exposure process described in the source. :contentReference[oaicite:71]{index=71} :contentReference[oaicite:72]{index=72} :contentReference[oaicite:73]{index=73}

## Important Limitations

- Self-guided CBT is presented as having limits. Severe symptoms, lack of safety, or failure to improve after several weeks of genuine practice are described as reasons to seek work with a therapist. :contentReference[oaicite:74]{index=74}
- The source states that self-guided CBT is most appropriate for mild difficulties, while severe or persistent symptoms are reasons to work with a trained therapist. :contentReference[oaicite:75]{index=75}
- The workbook is a self-help course rather than an accredited professional training program and does not provide a certificate. :contentReference[oaicite:76]{index=76}
- Professional treatment of other people using CBT requires formal clinical training and licensure according to the source. :contentReference[oaicite:77]{index=77}
- The source states that CBT research has often involved people completing self-help exercises while working with trained CBT therapists. :contentReference[oaicite:78]{index=78}
- Exposure should be adapted according to the anxiety disorder, and the source recommends professional guidance for severe anxiety. Response prevention is described as part of structured OCD treatment rather than a standalone technique for independent use. :contentReference[oaicite:79]{index=79}
- Emotional signals are described as fallible and should not automatically be treated as facts. :contentReference[oaicite:80]{index=80}
- Opposite action is intended to manage emotions that are too intense or prolonged; it is not presented as a method for denying or eliminating the importance of emotions. :contentReference[oaicite:81]{index=81}
- The source includes a specific caution that people with medical conditions potentially affected by slowed breathing or breath-holding should consult a doctor before using square breathing. :contentReference[oaicite:82]{index=82}
- The source explicitly states that the half-smile technique is not a substitute for medication or therapy. :contentReference[oaicite:83]{index=83}
